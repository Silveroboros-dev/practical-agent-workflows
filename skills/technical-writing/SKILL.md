# Technical Writing

Strengthen an authored technology argument without changing what the author means. Work from the supplied draft and any relevant source material. Ask about an ambiguity only when it would change the claim; otherwise make a bounded edit and state material assumptions.

1. **Find the central thesis first.** Identify what the author wants to show, which distinction matters, and why each example is there. Revise that understanding when the author clarifies their intent.
2. **Simplify expression while preserving scale.** Do not reduce a broad claim to an easier but narrower use case merely because it is easier to explain.
3. **Keep logical relationships exact.** Distinguish cause, condition, comparison, and analogy. An analogy that contrasts two ways of working does not imply that either way always produces the claimed outcome.
4. **Explain the concrete possibility.** If the draft describes a technological shift, show what a person can now do and how. Use metaphors only when they clarify the mechanism.
5. **Make examples serve the thesis.** Keep the example proportionate. A case study, ranking, or product anecdote should demonstrate the central possibility rather than become a different argument.
6. **Combine clear language with factual care.** Check precise attributions against primary sources when the task needs them. Separate quotation, paraphrase, and the author's own inference. Correct local inaccuracies directly; flag a problem that changes the thesis before rewriting it.

Deliver an edited passage or a small set of concrete alternatives, with a brief note on any material claim boundary. Preserve the author's language and voice unless they request a translation or different tone. Do not publish or send the text without authorization.
